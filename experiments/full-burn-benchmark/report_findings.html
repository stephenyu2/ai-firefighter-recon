1. Server-side, a whole burn takes seconds. All 1,353 Florida frames took 7.5 s inside the server on the RTX PRO 6000 with DINOv2-B and 12.4 s with DINOv2-g. The T4 needed 86 s for DINOv2-g. End-to-end times were 98 to 204 s, and most of that (58 to 95%) was uploading frames from a home connection.
  2. The GPU choice only matters for the large model. For DINOv2-g the GPU step is 4.1 ms per frame on the RTX, 25 ms on the L4 and 51 ms on the T4. For DINOv2-B every GPU is fast, and decoding and resizing the JPEG on the CPU (about 5 ms per frame) costs more than the model.
  3. On a site the detectors never saw, small fires get missed. Looking only at the thermal camera's view of each Florida frame, recall was 0.81 (DINOv2-B) and 0.79 (DINOv2-g). Every miss is a small fire covering under 0.5% of the thermal view, mostly early in the plot 2 burn. Given the whole RGB frame, recall rose to 0.986 and 0.994. Neither model flagged any of the 170 pre-burn frames.
  4. 4-bit is worth it for DINOv2-g on the RTX, not for DINOv2-B. Over the Florida burn, compiled NVFP4 cut DINOv2-g's GPU step from 3.6 to 2.0 ms per frame (server-side 8.4 to 6.9 ms) with the same or better recall on every set, changing 3 to 7 of 1,353 decisions. DINOv2-B got no faster and changed 25 to 35 decisions, so it stays in 16 bit. FP8 sits in between: 2.3 ms per frame for DINOv2-g with no changed decisions in the drift check.
  5. At 60 FPS the GPU is not the bottleneck. The 25-minute plot 1 burn would be about 90,700 frames. One RTX already keeps up with a live 60 FPS stream in 16 bit. NVFP4 pays off when one GPU serves several drones or works through recordings. Uploading 60 frames a second and decoding the JPEGs are bigger limits, and fire changes slowly enough that 1 to 2 frames per second carry the same information.
  6. Shrinking frames before upload cuts end-to-end time by about 65%, if the JPEG keeps full color. The detectors resize every frame to 308 by 252 anyway, so the sender can do it first. With default JPEG settings (quality 90, 4:2:0 color subsampling) the upload fell from 267 MB to 80 MB, but recall dropped (DINOv2-B 0.806 to 0.790, DINOv2-g 0.790 to 0.773) because subsampling blurs the small flames. Full-color JPEG (4:4:4) at quality 95 kept recall at 0.803 and 0.789 with a 115 MB upload, and cut end-to-end time from about 148 s to 51 to 54 s.
  7. Kai 0.6B adds speed of judgment, not accuracy. Served on an L4 (and on an RTX PRO 6000), Kai agreed with the detector it read on 98 to 99% of frames. It never raised recall: on the Florida crop it scored 0.798 against the detector's 0.806, and with the thermal reading added it still called all 155 small fires "none" when both image models said no fire. It takes about 31 ms per decision on the L4 and 17 ms on the RTX, one frame per request, so it fits once per brief, not on every frame.

Next steps this points to: keep fire status decided by the thermal rule, which sees the small fires the RGB detector misses, rather than by Kai; add burns like Florida's (small fires under tree canopy) to the training data, as the mentor suggested; prefer the full RGB frame over the thermal-view crop; send frames at 308 by 252 as full-color JPEG (4:4:4, quality 95); and move JPEG decode onto the GPU before reaching for lower precision.
