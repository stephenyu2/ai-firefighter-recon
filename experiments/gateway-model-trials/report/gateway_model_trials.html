CIS 5980 · Wildfire reconnaissance · Gateway model trials

Gateway Model Trials

Qwen3.8-27B at four precisions, each on a single RTX PRO 6000, three decision models, and an observer guidance prompt, all called through a LiteLLM gateway and scored on frames from Sycan Marsh ("Sycan") and Hanna Hammock ("Florida").

Summary

We tested which model should run the recon gateway's planning, image checks and writeup, whether a dedicated decision model (Kai 0.6B, Clef-Flash 9B or Clef 27B) should make the fire call instead of the LLM, and whether a generic observer guidance prompt helps models notice fire. Every call went through one LiteLLM gateway with fixed sampling, validated tool calls and logged runs; 600 full briefs and more than 8,000 single-frame checks completed with no failures. With thermal data every Qwen3.8-27B precision gets fire status right. The hard case is small fires seen in RGB only, where most models say no fire. The guidance prompt, written once and never tuned, raised single-frame recall on two separate frame samples for every Qwen3.8 build (FP8 only slightly) and for both Clef models, with no new false alarms. The measurement rule using guided BF16 image checks found 4 of 7 small RGB fires, against at most 1 for any model's own call.

What we recommend

  • Run the gateway LLM as Qwen3.8-27B, one build per RTX PRO 6000. Use BF16 with the observer guidance when image judgments matter most: guided BF16 has the best single-frame recall (0.53 and 0.48 on the two samples, against 0.43 and 0.42 for guided FP8), and the measurement rule on its image checks found 4 of 7 small RGB fires, against 2 of 7 with guided FP8. Use FP8 when speed matters: its writeup call is as accurate (42 of 60 briefs), it decodes 1.7 times faster and a brief takes 30 s instead of 43 s. Avoid INT4 for image judgments.
  • Keep the observer guidance on the per-frame image checks, where it raised recall on new frames with no false alarms. With the guidance on in the planner, image checks and writeup at once, the writeup's own call did not improve (BF16 fell from 42 to 39 of 60). Leave it out of the planner and writeup until a run with guidance on the image checks only confirms that split.
  • Keep thermal as the authority whenever a frame has it. For RGB-only footage, the measurement rule on three guided BF16 image checks found 4 of 7 small-fire sequences with no false alarms, against at most 1 for any model's own call (on guided FP8 or NVFP4 checks it found 2 of 7, and on INT4 none). Test it on more no-fire RGB footage before letting it override the writeup.
  • Do not make Kai, Clef or Clef-Flash the fire-status decider. From the measurements alone none of them caught a small RGB fire, and given the unguided BF16 image checks only Clef-Flash caught 1 of 7. Kai answers in about 0.1 s, so it may suit fast sub-questions such as confidence, but this report did not test that.
  • Small fires in RGB-only footage are the open gap. Add small, under-canopy fires like Florida plot 2 to the detector's training data, as the mentor suggested.

What we tested

In short

Five LLM routes (Qwen3.8-27B in BF16, FP8, INT4 and NVFP4, plus the earlier Qwen3-VL-4B), three decision models, 20 test sequences scored three times each, and two separate samples of single frames.

LLM routes. Qwen3.8-27B is a 27-billion-parameter model that reads images and text. A model's precision is how many bits store each number in it. Fewer bits (quantization) make the model smaller and faster but can cost accuracy. BF16 is the original 16-bit model (about 56 GB), FP8 the official 8-bit version (about 31 GB), INT4 stores weights in 4 bits but computes in 16 bits, and NVFP4 stores weights and activations in a 4-bit format the RTX PRO 6000 supports in hardware (about 26 GB). Each ran on its own RTX PRO 6000 with the same serving software (vLLM v0.31.0) and settings, except that BF16 also caps concurrent requests at 256 to fit in memory. The model's built-in step-by-step reasoning mode was off. The earlier gateway model, Qwen3-VL-4B on a smaller L4 GPU, is the baseline.

Decision models. Kai 0.6B reads text only. Clef 27B and Clef-Flash 9B read text plus images. All three answer fixed multiple-choice questions (fire, smoke only, none, unclear) with a probability for each option instead of writing text.

Test material. Twenty sequences of eight frames, scored three times each: 8 with thermal and 12 with RGB only, including 7 windows of small fires that the RGB detector misses. Florida fire truth comes from the thermal camera (any pixel at 80 C or above); pre-burn flights count as no fire because they were flown before ignition; Sycan truth comes from the dataset's fire and no-fire labels. Single-frame checks used 240 frames: Florida 40 large-fire, 40 small-fire, 60 pre-burn and 20 during-burn frames with no hot pixel, plus 40 Sycan fire and 40 Sycan no-fire frames. A second sample of 221 frames shares none of them; only 1 unused during-burn frame with no hot pixel was left for it.

How the gateway calls the models

In short

Every model call went through one LiteLLM gateway, and the tool-calling loop was rebuilt after two code reviews. Repeating a run almost always gave the same answer, invalid tool calls are rejected, and models see neutral ids instead of names that hint at the answer.

The gateway exposes one OpenAI-compatible endpoint for the five LLM routes and pass-through routes for the fire classifier, Kai, Clef and Clef-Flash. It logs every request with a run tag, its latency, token counts, finish reason and tool-call names. An unsupported request parameter is an error rather than being dropped silently.

Two code reviews of the gateway, with each reported problem confirmed by a second check before it was fixed, led to these changes before the final runs:

  • Answer scoring. A bug counted a whitespace token as the answer letter for "yes", which turned some fire answers into "no" and hit NVFP4 hardest. Fixing it raised NVFP4's single-frame ROC AUC (the ranking score explained below) from 0.78 to 0.90. INT4, FP8 and BF16 moved by 0.02 or less.
  • No hidden answers. Sequence and frame names such as "smallfire" or "preburn" reached the model in the first version. Models now see neutral ids (f01 to f08).
  • Fixed sampling. Temperature 0 and a fixed seed on every call, and one request at a time per model for scored single-frame checks.
  • Measurements first. The gateway runs the detector, thermal tools, spread tracking and three fixed image checks itself. The planner can only choose up to three more frames to check, with tool calls forced, frame ids limited to the real ones and every argument validated.
  • Strict output. The writeup must match a JSON schema, evidence is sent complete and in a fixed order, and the measured hot-area percentage is filled in by code instead of the model.
  • The measurement rule. This is code, not a model. With thermal, it calls fire when the thermal frames show a pixel at 80 C or above. Without thermal, it calls fire when the detector flags at least half the frames, or when at least 2 of the 3 fixed image checks (first, middle and last frame) say flames are visible with a yes-probability of 0.8 or more. Otherwise it calls no fire. Thermal data, or a detector that flags most frames, can overrule the LLM's writeup; weaker RGB-only evidence is recorded as a note instead.

Can each model see fire in one frame?

In short

On single frames, the Qwen3.8 builds rank fire frames well but answer "no" unless they see flames clearly. BF16 and FP8 judge best, INT4 and NVFP4 lose some, and no model raised a false alarm.

Each model got one RGB frame and the question "Are flames or glowing fire visible in this aerial image?" Recall is the share of fire frames the model answered yes to. ROC AUC measures how well the model's yes-probability ranks fire frames above no-fire frames, where 1.0 is a perfect ranking and 0.5 is chance. Large fires cover at least 0.5% of the thermal view at 80 C or more and small fires cover less, with 40 frames in each Florida fire group.

Does the observer guidance help?

In short

The observer guidance raised single-frame recall for every Qwen3.8 build (FP8 only slightly), and for both Clef models, on both samples, with no new false alarms. In full briefs it did not help the Qwen3.8 writeups' own call (BF16 fell from 42 to 39 of 60); only the old 4B improved, from 33 to 36.

The guidance is a short text, given to each LLM as a system message and appended to the question for Clef and Clef-Flash, describing what an aerial fire observer watches for (flame and glow, embers, burn edges, smoke rising from a source) and common look-alikes (bare soil, sun glint, haze, old burn scars). It was written once from general fire-observation practice, with no values from these two burns, and was not changed after seeing results. The second sample shares no frames with the first.

Who should decide fire status?

In short

The measurement rule was the most accurate fire call: 17 of 20 sequences on guided BF16 image checks and 15 of 20 on unguided ones, against 14 for the best Qwen3.8 writeup. The three decision models scored 11 to 13, mainly because they missed the small RGB fires.

Kai, Clef and Clef-Flash got the same measurements in words: detector results, a general image classifier's results, thermal results and camera motion. Clef and Clef-Flash also saw the first and last frames. A second pass also gave them the three fixed image checks from the unguided BF16 run (first repeat), so all three read identical evidence. On those same unguided checks the measurement rule scored 15 of 20, with 2 of 7 small fires. The decision models were not tested with the guided checks.

Method notes

  • Fire truth for the Florida burn sequences comes from the thermal camera (any pixel at 80 C or above), and on thermal sequences the gateway also uses that signal, so those results show the pipeline works rather than testing vision. Pre-burn sequences count as no fire because they were flown before ignition and have no thermal frames.
  • The brief comparison of precisions comes down to the 7 RGB-only small-fire sequences; on the other 13, every Qwen3.8 build is right. Read small differences there with care. FL-G is the same 8 frames as FL-B with thermal withheld.
  • The guided brief arm turned the guidance on in three places at once. The single-frame test isolates the image-check placement and is the cleaner evidence.
  • The image-check part of the measurement rule was tested for false alarms on only 4 RGB-only no-fire sequences (FL-D, FL-N, FL-O, FL-P). On the two thermal no-fire sequences (FL-E and SY-2_7), thermal decides. The rule needs more no-fire RGB footage before it should overrule a writeup.
  • Clef and Clef-Flash ran on their reference Python code, which is slower than an optimized server, so their timings are upper bounds.
  • Single-frame checks sent one request at a time per model, with temperature 0 and a fixed seed, so a rerun reproduced every answer. Batching requests on a server can shift answers slightly.
  • Sequences FL-H to FL-P were added after a review found that only one sequence separated the models. They were chosen by frame number: the small-fire ones from the thermal-labeled plot 2 small-fire range, and the no-fire ones from the pre-burn flights. None were chosen from model results.

Appendix: the observer guidance prompt

The full text, used unchanged in every guided run. It contains nothing specific to the two test burns.

You are reviewing aerial imagery for a wildfire reconnaissance team. Fires seen from the air are often small,
partly hidden, or low intensity, so look at the whole image, including the edges and the ground under tree canopy.

Signs of active fire:
- Flame or glow: orange, yellow or red light at the edge of vegetation. In low-intensity surface fires this may be only
  small points or a thin broken line, not tall flames.
- Embers: bright orange or red spots on dark ground.
- A burn edge: the boundary between unburned vegetation and freshly blackened ground. Active fire usually sits on that
  boundary. Fresh char is black; older ash is gray or white.
- Smoke with a source: smoke rising from a point or a line on the ground. Follow a plume back to its base; the base is
  where fire is most likely. Thin wisps coming up through a canopy can be the only visible sign of a surface fire.
- Recently scorched vegetation: brown or orange-brown crowns next to blackened ground.

Common look-alikes that are not active fire on their own:
- Bare soil, sand, dirt roads, dry grass, and red or orange foliage: color without light, char or smoke.
- Sun glint on water, metal, vehicles or equipment, and lens flare.
- Haze, dust, fog or cloud with no ground source.
- Old burn scars: uniformly black or gray ground with no smoke, glow or flame.
- People, vehicles, buildings and equipment, including orange or red gear.

Weigh the evidence: flame, glow or embers are direct evidence. Fresh smoke rising from a ground source is strong
evidence of fire nearby even when no flame is visible. Diffuse haze with no source is weak evidence.
